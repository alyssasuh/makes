OVERVIEW

The scope of bioengineering can span many scales– but what happens when we focus on fabrication at the level of individual cells? For my final project in Cellular Engineering, I investigated collective cell migration during gap closure while building a toolkit of lab techniques along the way. Cellular Engineering is a rigorous, graduate-level undergraduate class focused on manipulating cell behavior with modern lab techniques, which I took during my senior year at Harvard. This page documents the skills I gained and the final project exploring gap closure across varying geometries.

LAB TECHNIQUES

Throughout the course we gained hands-on experience with fundamental tissue culture techniques, primarily working with human normal dermal fibroblasts (HNDF) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC). Our team was responsible for maintaining both lines throughout the semester, ensuring they were healthy and ready for each experiment.

WOUND HEALING ASSAY

We first performed a wound healing (scratch) assay, allowing both cell lines to grow to full confluency before creating a scratch. The wounds were imaged over three days at 40x magnification to quantify the rate of wound closure. The carousel captures HNDF behavior over 56 hours, highlighting progressive migration and gap closure.

SUBSTRATE & STAMP MAKING

To culture cells on micropatterned surfaces, we prepared a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mixture that served as both the coverslip coating and the mold material. Desired geometries were etched onto a 3" silicon wafer in the cleanroom, cleaned with isopropanol, and dried with nitrogen. We poured PDMS over the wafer, degassed it, and spin-coated the same mixture onto glass coverslips before curing both overnight at 65 °C.

Once cured, the PDMS stamps were carefully detached from the wafers, trimmed to size, and paired with their matching substrates.

BSA-FITC dye tests confirmed proper stamp formation and transfer for three single-cell shapes (square, triangle, circle) plus two line widths (10 μm, 20 μm).

MICROPATTERNING CELLS

Using the validated stamps, we microcontact printed fibronectin patterns onto PDMS-coated coverslips. Stamps were inked, dried, and pressed to transfer the protein, followed by Pluronic F-127 treatment to block non-patterned regions. HNDF cells were seeded and allowed to adhere for 24 hours before imaging.

FINAL PROJECT

Our final project explored how cell type, anisotropy, and geometric constraints influence collective gap closure in HNDFs and HUVECs. We hypothesized that HNDFs would migrate faster due to their elongated morphology and stress fiber organization, and that higher-curvature gaps would close more rapidly. Custom resin molds were fabricated to cast PDMS stamps that defined circular, square, and triangular gaps with equal surface areas (30 mm²).

Stamps were applied to fibronectin-coated substrates prepared with isotropic and anisotropic alignment patterns. Over 72 hours we observed that HUVECs closed gaps faster than HNDFs, while isotropic substrates and sharp-curvature geometries produced the highest migration rates. These findings underscore how cell type, alignment, and curvature collectively influence wound closure dynamics, informing future tissue-engineering strategies.
